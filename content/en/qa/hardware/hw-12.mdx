**Unlike the iPhone, current iPad models (including the iPad 9th gen and USB-C based models) do not have an 'externally visible' Liquid Contact Indicator (LCI).**

The determination of liquid damage is made by authorized technicians after disassembling the device to inspect internal indicators.

## How Service Centers Determine Liquid Damage:

### 1. Internal LCI Inspection

- During a **Visual Mechanical Inspection (VMI)** at an Apple Authorized Service Provider (AASP), technicians check LCI stickers placed on the logic board or near critical flex cables.
- If the sticker has turned **red** due to liquid contact, the device is classified as having liquid damage.

### 2. External Secondary Indicators

Even if internal stickers aren't visible, technicians look for:

- Corrosion (green or white powdery residue) inside the charging port (Lightning/USB-C) or speaker meshes.
- Liquid residue or "water spots" visible behind the screen assembly.
- Odors or residue near the microphone or headphone jack.

### 3. Warranty Impact

- Liquid damage is not covered under the standard Apple one-year limited warranty and is treated as **Accidental Damage**.
- If localized repair is impossible, a paid unit replacement is typically required.

## Expert Strategy:

- **Storage Environment**: Avoid storing devices in unventilated, high-humidity rooms or basements during school breaks. Long-term exposure to extreme humidity can trigger internal LCIs even without a direct spill.
- **Safe Cleaning**: **Never spray liquid directly** onto the iPad. Capillary action can instantaneously pull moisture inside through the button seams or speaker grilles, leading to immediate warranty denial.
- **Reporting**: If an iPad fails to power on after a spill, be transparent with the service provider. Keeping high-value hardware dry and properly documented is a vital frontline defense for minimizing out-of-warranty repair costs.
